Voice-profile corpus — what each build actually costs

Measured constants, three inference engines, eight build scenarios, against both compute budgets. Profile B (the Mediathek merge) is dropped — standard profile only.

Read this first. Every number below rests on measurements, not estimates: 2,126 generations per GPU-hour and a 8.83 s mean clip from the 323,200-generation demo; 288 core-hours per node-hour and 4 GPUs per node read straight off Slurm accounting; the engine speeds from the SGLang-Omni benchmark; and the quality-versus-N figures from an exact order-statistic analysis of real 200-candidate pools.

The single biggest lever is not N — it is the inference engine. The current pipeline runs at 5.2× realtime per GPU. Plain pipelined decode in the same stack reaches 18.8×, and SGLang-Omni 31.2×. That is a 3.6–6× cost difference on every row of the table.

Top-1 or top-3? What N buys, precisely

The table above answers "how good is the single best take out of N". If you keep more than one take per group that is the wrong question, so both are computed here — exactly, using order statistics over the real 200-candidate pools (807 groups), not by sampling.

For a pool of M sorted candidates, the expected j-th largest of a random subset of size N is Σ v(i)·C(i−1,N−j)·C(M−i,j−1) / C(M,N). Percentages are relative to N=8 → N=200.

Two results, one of them a clean rule.
  • The shape of the curve does not change. Top-1 and mean-of-top-3 track each other almost exactly (27/51/72/90 % versus 28/52/72/90 %). So whether you keep one take or three does not change which N is worth paying for — the scenario recommendations are unaffected.
  • But the absolute quality of what you keep does drop, by a clean factor: the 3rd best of N is worth about the same as the best of N/4. Read the last column — the 3rd best at N=128 equals the top-1 at N=32, the 3rd best at N=64 equals the top-1 at N=16, and so on. It holds for speaker similarity too.
Practical consequence. If all three kept takes go into the corpus as equals, the effective quality is the mean of the top-3, not the max — at N=32 that is 0.4655 against 0.5211 for a single best-of-32, with the weakest kept take at 0.4181. To make your worst kept take as good as a single best-of-N would have been, budget 4× the candidates.

Recommendation: keep top-1 per group for the training corpus — every other candidate is stored anyway, so nothing is lost and re-ranking stays free. Use top-3 only where alternates are genuinely wanted, such as the character-cluster groups where the standard and voice-converted renderings are meant to be compared and annotated. Raising N across all 832 groups to compensate for keeping three would cost 4× and would not improve the best take at all.

Recommendation

  1. Port pipelined decode first. It is the same transformers stack, LoRA adapters keep working, and it is a measured 3.6×. Everything else is downstream of this.
  2. Then S5 (5,000×16 + 900×32 + 100×128). Broad coverage where speaker similarity is already 74 % saturated, a solid middle tier, and a 100-voice high-quality core for the work that needs it — at a fraction of a flat build.
  3. Treat SGLang-Omni as a follow-up, not a plan. It is 6× faster in the benchmark but nothing has yet shown it can hot-swap LoRA adapters at arbitrary merge strength, which the entire condition matrix depends on. Confirm that before counting on it.
  4. Always pack 4 GPUs per node. Slurm bills the whole node either way; one GPU per node quadruples every figure above.

Assumptions and where they are weak

  • The engine speeds are not apples-to-apples. The benchmark ran different clip lengths, different languages and no director instruction. The doc's own honest number is 1.47× SGLang over pipelined transformers; the 5.2× baseline is ours, measured on the real workload with scoring included. Treat the middle column as solid and the right-hand column as indicative.
  • Voice conversion for the character groups is costed at 2.5 s per clip for the top-3 only (~0.05 GPU-h/voice). Converting every candidate instead would add roughly 0.5 GPU-h per voice.
  • Quality fractions come from one voice's 200-candidate pools (832 groups). Group-to-group variance is real; treat them as central estimates.
  • Scoring is 4.8 % of the loop and is folded into the 2,126 gen/GPU-h figure. A faster generator makes scoring a proportionally larger share, so the 18.8×/31.2× rows are slightly optimistic — the scoring stage does not speed up.
  • No listener study. "Quality" throughout is the automatic sensor stack, which agrees with a human at roughly ρ ≈ +0.21 on spoken material.
