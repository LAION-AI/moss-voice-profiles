Assembled voice-acting scenes — raw, voice-converted, restored

9 scenes × 8 assemblies each. Every clip is a complete performance spliced from 2–4 separately generated takes. 160 kbps.

What you are listening to. Each scene was written as a short dramatic arc — a confrontation, a confession, a panic — and performed as several separate takes that are then spliced into one continuous ~15 second scene. The hard part is not any single take; it is that all the takes have to sound like the same person and the joins must not be audible.
The three columns are not three qualities of the same thing.
raw — straight out of the generator.
+ VC, per part — what the production pipeline does: each part converted toward the anchor separately, then spliced. Changes who the voice sounds like.
+ VC, whole clip — the assembled scene converted in one pass. A listener reported the per-part version still sounds fragmented, and the code confirms why: each part is converted with no knowledge of its neighbours, so the converter cannot smooth a seam it never sees. Compare these two columns directly — and listen for whether the one-pass version also flattens prosodic changes that were meant to differ across a join.
+ Sidon — speech restoration (w2v-BERT encoder, DAC decoder, 48 kHz out, loudness normalised). This changes how clean it sounds and does not target a speaker at all.

So do not expect Sidon to fix identity drift, or Chatterbox to fix noise. They answer different questions, and the most common mistake with this page is reading them as a quality ladder.
Sidon cost, measured. 72 assemblies, 2017 s of audio restored in 97 s of GPU time — 20.7× realtime, output at 48000 Hz. At that rate restoring a million 15-second scenes costs roughly 201 GPU-hours.

How to judge these

  • Speaker similarity is the cosine to the anchor. Worst part matters more than the mean: one part drifting is what makes an assembly sound like two actors, and the mean hides it.
  • DNSMOS raw → VC shows what voice conversion cost in audio quality. On assemblies that were already close to the anchor it is roughly neutral; on badly drifted ones it has been measured to cost about −0.109, so conversion is not free where it is most needed.
  • Listen for the joins. The measurements say nothing about whether a splice is audible. Tempo, chunking and disfluency must either flow smoothly across a seam or change for a reason the scene justifies.
No human has scored any of this. Every number here comes from automatic scorers whose agreement with a listener on spoken material is about ρ ≈ +0.21. Treat the rankings as a way to find interesting clips, not as a verdict.
